Hi! My name is Jane Goleva. I gave a [talk about feedback at TeamLeadConf](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Eqtzj2JIJQw), and I'd like to share a loose transcript of it with you. I managed to sneak feedback training into a completely different project and teach our engineers to give much better feedback than they had before. To do that, I spent a long time carefully explaining why and how to give feedback. Then I gave them plenty of chances to practice, keeping a watchful eye on them and offering gentle guidance.

I made plenty of mistakes and reinvented quite a few wheels along the way. I hope some of the less obvious insights and methods will be useful to anyone who wants to introduce a healthy feedback culture to their team.

Today I'm speaking as someone who has been involved in adult learning for more than ten years. I know very well that feedback is the main tool for learning and motivation. Why we need it, what kinds there are, and how I managed to teach employees to give it properly — that's the subject of my talk today.

Our company has 4,500 full-time employees, 300 of whom are IT specialists. [Why do we need so many?](https://habr.com/ru/companies/lamoda/articles/426501/) The answer is simple: almost all of [Lamoda's software development is done in-house](https://latech.ru/). We automate the operations of a huge warehouse, delivery to 600 cities across Russia, three call centers and our own photo studio. All these operations run on systems we develop in-house because we couldn't find suitable solutions on the market.

And, of course, we often run into a familiar problem: many of these specialists either don't give their colleagues any feedback at all or give it in a way that isn't quite what we'd like. I'll try to explain why that happens, why it's a problem and how we can fix it.

## What motivates engineers?

First, I'd like to say a few words about why I pay so much attention to feedback skills in our engineering teams. What motivates engineers at work? For our engineers at Lamoda, it's important to build cool things, make their own decisions along the way and ultimately earn recognition from their peers. Dan Pink, a well-known business consultant and author of books about modern approaches to motivation in business, describes the motivation of knowledge workers in much the same way in his [talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/dan_pink_the_puzzle_of_motivation).

Whether engineers feel recognized by their peers depends largely on the quality of the feedback they receive.

But let's be honest: how does someone without any particular training usually give feedback? They criticize often, rarely praise and pass judgment without fully understanding the situation. It turns out that this kind of feedback isn't especially motivating for others, and it often leads to conflict as well.

An engineer who constantly receives this sort of feedback from their team lead reacts predictably: without positive feedback, they conclude that they're not valued. Without developmental feedback, they feel they have no room to grow.

And what do they do in that situation? They say, "I'm off!"

The consequences are clear: while we're looking for a replacement, projects are on fire, everyone else's workload increases and the company spends a lot of money finding a new team member.

So it matters a great deal what kind of feedback our engineers receive. That means the people giving it (mostly team leads in this case) need to know how to do it properly. Why, then, is it so often the case that team leads don't know how to give good feedback?

## How do people become team leads?

It's great when a company deliberately develops its team leads, giving them the chance to acquire all the skills they need to manage a team before taking on a leadership position. But sometimes what happens is that the person who writes the best code and understands the system best is chosen and put in charge of that system and a team.

As a result, you can lose an excellent engineer and end up with a demotivated team lead who's struggling to do the job.

There's only one way to avoid this: deliberately teach prospective (and existing) team leads how to work with a team. But that turns out not to be so simple either.

## How do we teach team leads to manage people?

The usual way to prepare team leads is to send them to a training session. More often than not, it's a one-off. It's important to understand that giving good feedback is a skill that can only be developed through practice. Unfortunately, a single training session just won't do it. At best, people will acquire some theoretical knowledge, and they'll have to learn to apply it on their own in their actual work.

They can bring the questions that inevitably arise to a follow-up session, but such sessions aren't always organized, and attendance tends to be low.

When people learn to give feedback on the job, there's an additional difficulty: they're still learning, but we can't see how they're doing. That's because, in actual practice, team leads usually give employees feedback privately in a one-to-one meeting. We can't correct them, offer suggestions or give feedback on their feedback because it's all happening behind closed doors.

While I was thinking about how to get around this, I had an idea: what if I wrapped feedback training into another project and ran it as a club?

## Why is a club a better format for learning to give feedback?

1. Attendance is voluntary, which means the people who come are genuinely motivated to learn.
2. Unlike a training session, a club isn't a one-off event. People attend regularly over a long period, so they can not only acquire theoretical knowledge but also learn to apply it.
3. In a club, we can oversee the learning process. A person gives feedback not in a private one-to-one conversation but in a practice situation observed by the other club members. We can therefore give them feedback on their feedback and help them improve the skill.

*The original talk also contrasted the practical trade-offs: a training session is quick, while a club takes time; both require investment.*

## Speakers Club: learning to speak in public and give feedback

How exactly did we put feedback training into practice at Lamoda? Feedback is always about something: there needs to be some work that's been done for others to respond to. So feedback training can be combined with another activity.

At the time, as a DevRel specialist, I was working on my main objective: I needed our experts to start giving regular talks at well-known IT conferences. Many of our specialists understood that they needed to improve their public speaking skills to do that. Largely at my colleagues' suggestion, I organized a speakers club at the company (Lamoda Speakers Club).

But as part of Speakers Club, we also worked on feedback skills. I taught people to give one another feedback in a way that would actually be useful to them. And I helped them experience for themselves why receiving good feedback matters so much.

## What is Lamoda Speakers Club?

The club is a place where you can do three things:

1. Speak in a safe environment.
2. Make mistakes.
3. Experiment.

Here's how it works. Each participant prepares a short talk on any subject (five minutes for the talk and another five for questions). After the participant gives the talk, the audience gives them feedback. The club meets every two weeks, with no more than six speakers per meeting (an hour for talks and questions, another hour for feedback). Participation is voluntary.

## What is good feedback, and how do you sell the idea to others?

As I said at the beginning of the talk, most people initially don't make the best use of feedback. Instead of being a powerful motivational tool, it becomes something extremely unpleasant for both the person giving it and the person receiving it. This happens because people don't know enough about how motivation works or what actually helps others do their jobs better.

So my main objective wasn't just to tell my colleagues what good feedback looks like. I also had to sell them on the idea, so they could see that this unfamiliar way of giving feedback genuinely worked better than the approach they were used to.

So what does good feedback look like? What idea are we selling?

Let's look at the different kinds of feedback.

### 1. Positive and negative

Positive feedback answers the question "What went well?" Negative feedback answers the question "What went badly?"

This was where I had to get across to our engineers that positive feedback is absolutely essential for motivation. But simply telling someone what went badly doesn't help them improve. That's why we use **developmental feedback** instead: it answers the question "What could be improved?"

### 2. Useful or not particularly useful

What makes feedback useful?

It has to be **specific and constructive**. It helps the person understand what to keep doing and what to do differently.

To give constructive feedback, we learned to distinguish our personal judgments and feelings from facts. We're all human, so feelings are present even in technical discussions.

### 3. No feedback at all

Yes, that's a kind of feedback too, and club members really came to appreciate its significance through public speaking. Imagine this: someone spends half an hour preparing, goes on stage, gives a talk — and gets absolutely nothing in response. No questions, no comments, no objections. At that moment, it suddenly dawns on them that getting no feedback hurts. It's awful.

That was probably my first important discovery: it turned out to be much easier to convey the need for feedback at Speakers Club than to explain it ten times in words.

For some reason, a person who receives no feedback immediately assumes they've done something badly. Very few people think they've done a great job anyway when there's deathly silence in the room. So if we want to motivate our employees, we can't just leave them wondering how they're doing. Our club members know what that feels like.

> Feedback must be given. It must include both positive and developmental elements, and it must be useful — that is, constructive.

Of course, this view of feedback is very different from what many people are used to: if everything's fine, you don't have to say anything, but if something's wrong, you need to criticize it without bothering to offer constructive suggestions. So I met with resistance from some colleagues and had to sell them on the idea of good feedback — in other words, show them in practice that it works much better.

Let me tell you about the main reservations club members had and how I worked with them.

### Problem: fear of hurting someone's feelings

This was the first thing I encountered. People are afraid to make both positive and negative comments. The idea that feedback is meant to help people grow rather than hit them over the head isn't a very familiar one in our culture. So most people are simply scared of both giving and receiving feedback.

**Solution: lead by example and give people time to get used to it.**

### Problem: excessive aggression

Often, when someone gives feedback, you get the impression they're trying to make themselves look good at someone else's expense. It goes something like this: "Now I'm going to tell you how not to do it!" And there they are, looking terribly pleased with themselves, as if they're the smartest person in the room. Then they're surprised that people won't listen to them or accept their ideas.

When I encountered someone like this, it was important for me to remember that they were actually a good person. They weren't there to make everyone's life miserable; they were just trying to stand up for the truth.

Their problem was simply that they didn't yet know how to communicate their thoughts properly. My job was to teach them.

**Solution: we're all equals here. And if you haven't given a talk, you don't give feedback.**

I reminded everyone that we'd all come to the club to get better at something none of us had mastered: public speaking. Anyone could make mistakes, and everyone's opinion mattered equally. We used feedback at the club not to find out who was better, but to share experience and work out ways of solving a problem together. There was no need to tell everyone else they'd done a terrible job.

That led to another rule: if you haven't given a talk, you don't give feedback. Once someone has been in the speaker's shoes, they understand what it's like to receive feedback in front of the group. Their feedback immediately sounds different and becomes much more constructive and useful.

### Problem: reluctance to give positive feedback

Some people believe that positive feedback isn't needed at all. One of our team leads came to me and said something along these lines: "I want to give talks, but I don't want to give other people feedback."

**Solution: demonstrate the effectiveness of positive feedback.**

Being fair in your assessment means recognizing what worked as well as what could be improved. If all you ever point out are shortcomings, you're not giving the person a complete picture of their work. And that kind of feedback doesn't do much to motivate them.

In situations like this, I like to tell the story of someone who used to be a team lead and later became a CTO. When he did code reviews, he left lots of comments about things that needed fixing, and people became resistant to his feedback. Somewhere, he read an article about the power of positive feedback, which advised telling people what they had done well. So he started adding one or two positive comments to every code review.

As a result, people began to look forward to his comments with genuine curiosity, because they felt that their work had been looked at and assessed fairly.

### Problem: reluctance to receive positive feedback

Sometimes the person receiving feedback doesn't want to hear about their strengths, considering it a waste of time. This is connected with the culture of devaluing achievements that exists in the post-Soviet world. Unfortunately, when someone tells us we've done something well, we let it go in one ear and out the other. But the moment someone points out a shortcoming, we take out a big magnifying glass and look at it. In other words, we focus only on our problems and fail to notice our successes.

I tried to show club members in practice exactly how they could use the positive feedback they received, to help them appreciate its importance.

**Solution 1: positive feedback helps test hypotheses.**

You can use positive feedback to test hypotheses. For example, when preparing a talk, you think a particular fact will really resonate with the audience. But during the feedback session, you notice that when people list the good things about your work, not one of them mentions it. Perhaps it wasn't as powerful an argument as you'd thought. Next time, try emphasizing something else.

**Solution 2: positive feedback helps you understand where to invest effort and where not to.**

Here's an example from our club. One of our department heads has a very quiet voice, and he used to struggle to project it. He worked on this for quite a long time and finally managed it. He got to the point where he could speak to the whole room without a microphone. Over three club meetings in a row, people told him they could hear him perfectly well, wherever they were sitting. That's it. At that point, he can stop working on it and move on to developing the next skill, while remembering to build on his strengths.

**Solution 3: positive feedback helps you improve your craft.**

There are two approaches to development:

1. Working on your weaknesses: identify them and try to improve them.
2. Building on your strengths: develop the skills you're already good at until they're strong enough to compensate for your weaker ones.

Suppose you already know you're not particularly good at planning a public talk, but you're excellent at improvising when interacting with people. Fine, don't plan your talk down to the last detail. Don't torture yourself. Prepare the key points and then improvise. Why force yourself to work in a way that drains your motivation when you could play to your strengths? But to do what you're best at, you first need to know what that is.

### Problem: unbalanced feedback

I'll never tire of repeating this: good feedback is an extremely powerful tool for helping us do better work. But for it to work, it has to be balanced. It must mention both what went well and what could (or should) be improved. As you can see, people found it hard to give positive feedback, so the feedback overall wasn't particularly balanced.

**Solution 1: three positives or stay quiet.**

To make the most of positive feedback, we introduced another rule at the club: if you couldn't find three things that genuinely worked in a talk and explain them, you had to stay quiet. This motivated people to find positives so they could have their say. Our feedback became more balanced as a result.

**Solution 2: don't make excuses.**

You need to know how to accept positive feedback. Here, again because of our culture of devaluing achievements, people often start making excuses when their work is praised. "Oh, it just happened by chance. Guys, it wasn't even my doing." And then, "Look at all the things I can see wrong with my own work."

That's not constructive. You got up there and gave that talk. People will tell you what they saw in your work. Those are your achievements. Accept them. If something didn't turn out very well, you don't need to mention it immediately, even if nobody noticed it. There's no need to inspect your shortcomings with a magnifying glass. It's much more useful to look at the whole picture: what worked and what didn't.

As you can see, this entire section was mostly about selling the idea of positive feedback, because people find it very difficult to accept.

## Section recap: what helps me sell the idea of good feedback?

- Leading by example.
- Treating everyone as equals.
- Showing both the giver and the recipient why balanced feedback matters, especially the part about what went well.

So far, I've talked about which kind of feedback is most useful and how to convince others of that. Now I want to explain how I designed the learning process itself to teach people to give exactly that kind of feedback.

## How to teach people to give more specific feedback

Feedback very often lacks specificity. For example, someone tells you that you've done a great job and got everything right. Okay, but what exactly did I do well? From that feedback it's not very clear what I should keep doing to get good results in the future. If you can't explain specifically what was good about someone's work, your feedback isn't specific.

*Examples from the original slides: “Good”, “Bad”, “All over the place”, “Weak”, “Interesting”, “Great job!”*

How did I tackle this problem?

### 1. Define the assessment tools and criteria

We agreed that we would have three broad categories for assessing a public talk, with further criteria within each category.

| 1. Structure |
| 2. Delivery |
| 3. Audience interaction |

Later, we held a similar meeting with the department heads and the CTO about what, exactly, we were assessing our engineers on. And you know what? We spent an hour or two aligning those assessment criteria and our expectations. Before we established these criteria, it was rather difficult to say whether people were being assessed in the same way across different departments.

### 2. Capture the details

I suggested that participants make notes while listening to other people's talks. If you want to give specific feedback on someone's talk, write down the exact phrases or arguments that worked for you and those that didn't. Specific feedback requires details, and it's impossible to remember them even after just two or three talks in a row.

If you don't take notes as the testers, analysts and developers on your team go about their work, you won't remember the details later, and you won't be able to give specific feedback.

### 3. Speak for yourself

Sometimes people present their personal impressions as objective facts: "That argument wasn't convincing."

Hold on. It didn't convince **you**. Why exactly? Say "I wasn't convinced by that argument" and explain why. And don't assume everyone else felt the same way.

You might have found the argument unconvincing, but others may have a different opinion. Ask them! That's what I taught people to do: speak for themselves and check whether others share their impressions.

### 4. Take an opinion into account without having to accept it

You don't have to act on every piece of feedback as if it were the absolute truth. No. Some of it is just one person's opinion. Perhaps they're an expert or your manager, in which case you need to take their opinion into account, but that doesn't mean you have to agree with all of it.

This is another part of the culture of safety we built within the club: you're allowed to acknowledge feedback without acting on it. It's still your decision what to change in your talk and what to leave alone.

### 5. Take the person's level into account

We have a tester who's been with the company for six years and has tested every internal system that has ever existed here. He's an excellent tester, so he put together a 28-point checklist for assessing public talks and went through it every time.

It turned out not to be very useful, because after all, you need to take into account the level of the person you're giving feedback to. We'd initially agreed on three categories of assessment. For beginners, the first category is where we start: the structure of the talk. A beginner may not yet know who they're speaking to or what exactly they want to say. The ending may be unclear, and so on.

If the speaker hasn't figured that out yet, why tell them they're not interacting enough with the audience? That feedback may be perfectly valid, but it's not what they need at this stage. In learning, it's important to move in sequence, taking small steps. So I asked our tester to stick to our three categories and give feedback based on the level of the speaker whose talk he was assessing.

And he started making deeper, more interesting suggestions about how things could be improved. His feedback became more useful because it focused on the most important and readily achievable improvements.

### 6. The three-plates rule

Imagine getting three positive observations and three suggestions for improvement from each of five people. That's a lot to work with!

If you try to incorporate everything into your next talk, you'll end up like a juggler trying to keep too many plates spinning at once.

Instead, pick just two or three skills to work on for your next talk. Once you've made progress with those, move on to another set.

*The original slide connects this rule to the zone of proximal development.*

### 7. Build the expertise needed to give useful feedback

"Don't look at the slides behind you," says the person giving feedback. And they get a perfectly logical response: "What should I do instead?" The intention is good. But the speaker may not have enough experience to figure out what to do instead, and the person giving feedback may not know either. That's why part of teaching people to give constructive feedback is helping them develop their own expertise.

How do you build that expertise?

1. Share your own experience.
2. Draw on the participants' experience.

I'm not claiming to be some public speaking guru myself. I have a little more experience than each participant individually, but five participants together have more experience than I do. So I ask, "How do you do this? What would you do in this situation? If you were preparing this talk, what objective would you set yourself?" Everyone walks away with not only their own answers but the answers of the other participants too.

My role here is to validate ideas. Sometimes what works brilliantly for one person won't work for others. Or I know that a suggestion might do harm. In that case, I give my opinion, but I leave the decision to the participants.

And so another rule developed in our club: **if you don't know, ask for advice.** I really like it because, for example, if someone doesn't know what topic to speak about, they can simply come to our Speakers Club group chat and ask, "How do you deal with X? What do you do if Y happens?" Because we've managed to build a safe atmosphere within the group, people aren't afraid to ask for advice and raise questions, even if those questions seem silly.

### To sum up: what helps people learn?

The approach we arrived at for developing feedback skills can be written down very briefly as follows:

1. Only someone who has given a talk themselves gives feedback.
2. Start by naming three positive things — what went well.
3. Name three things that could be improved.
4. Use the assessment criteria we've agreed on and take the speaker's skill level into account.
5. Work on three skills at a time, no more.
6. Share your experience and draw on other people's experience.
7. Most importantly, make sure every speaker gets feedback.

## What Speakers Club achieved for participants and the company

Before coming to this conference, I asked the people in our club, "Do the skills you've acquired at Speakers Club help you at work?" Here's the feedback I received from club members:

1. It's become easier to communicate ideas to the team.
2. Giving developmental rather than negative feedback is a huge help with junior colleagues and new employees.
3. Using positive feedback genuinely helps motivate the team.
4. These skills come in handy in other meetings, too: they help people structure their responses and address what the other person actually needs to know.

My favorite response came from our head of ERP development: "Now people sometimes come and ask for feedback themselves." I think all these responses are a very important indication that people have genuinely learned something.

If you're not getting enough feedback from your manager, colleagues or direct reports, try asking for it outright. Sometimes a face-to-face conversation is enough. If it's important to you to receive more specific feedback, structure your request. Write an email asking your manager, colleague or team lead to answer particular questions. Make sure you state what's important to you and what you would like to discuss with your manager. Perhaps not all managers have developed the skill of giving high-quality feedback — that's okay.

But if you have that skill yourself, you'll be much better equipped to get useful feedback, even from someone who hasn't learned how to give it.

**Giving feedback culture a foothold.** Yes, not everyone attends our club regularly. Some people came along to see what it was about and never returned. But even those who didn't stay have had a chance to see what better feedback looks like, and can pass that on to others. People learn by example. If there are lots of examples of specific, developmental feedback, the feedback culture will spread. Especially if those examples come from people whose judgment others respect.

## How to teach feedback skills within your own team

Of course, you can teach feedback skills in some other setting rather than using a speakers club. It's great if you already have ideas! What matters is that the following criteria are met:

1. **The right environment.** A safe space where people have the right to make mistakes and the opportunity to experiment.
2. **A new, interesting topic for the main discussion.** This could be anything: a new technology, a new practice or a methodology.
3. **Make it easy for newcomers to join.** So that you can bring new participants on board at any point, you need to maintain equality and make sure a newcomer's opinion is heard and taken into account just as much as the opinions of long-standing members.
4. **Duration and regularity.** I'll say it again: giving good feedback is a difficult skill. You can't teach it quickly. I noticed that by about the third time they gave feedback, the engineers in our club had learned to spot what worked. By about the sixth time, they were starting to get the hang of being specific, useful and constructive. People need to practice constantly to learn to do this properly.
5. **Feedback on feedback.** There must be someone to guide and correct how people practice their feedback skills. At first, I gave feedback on the public talks. Then club members started doing that themselves, and I only gave feedback on their feedback. In other words, if you want to lead this project or club, you'll also have to take on the facilitator's role and help people acquire the skill.

So when it comes to teaching people to give feedback, I think the club wins hands down. What do you think? Could you organize a club like this at your company?
