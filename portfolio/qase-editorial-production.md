# Content Operations for Technical Thought Leadership at Qase (B2B SaaS)

*Building an audience-driven editorial production system for expert-led content, with AI-assisted quality assurance.*

**Jane Goleva · Editorial Producer · 2026**

I also designed and built an interactive presentation documenting the pilot, including its narrative, page structure, diagrams and visuals.

**Pilot results:** 5 source packages, 17 distinct content pieces and 41 finished video deliverables in three weeks. Production turnaround: one to two weeks, compared with previous delays of up to six months.

At Qase, a test management platform, our goal was to build a relevant professional audience around the company and its developer advocate. An audience we could have an ongoing, meaningful conversation with.

## Six months for a short? Let's make it two weeks.

And we already had plenty of expert content to work with: regular thought-leadership videos, a community podcast, meetup recordings and videos featuring internal experts.

LinkedIn was working well for the developer advocate, and there was potential to expand distribution through the company's own channels and YouTube.

The problem was getting from a recording to something we could actually distribute. Sometimes it took up to six months.

Was editing itself taking six months? No. The video editor was actually quite fast. But someone had to watch the original recording, decide what was worth cutting, provide timestamps and review the result. Neither the Developer Advocate nor the Head of Content had enough time for that.

And technical content requires quite a bit of editorial attention. Goodhart's law, for example, can easily turn into something about a good heart in automatically generated subtitles.

I joined Qase to get short-form content ready for distribution within one or two weeks and take editorial production off the team's plates.

## Audience-driven content repurposing: which five stories do we produce?

Suppose we have 30 potential fragments and can produce only five. Which five do we choose?

Of course, we need a good hook, a coherent argument, something useful for the viewer and footage that can actually be edited. But I wanted another selection criterion: relevance to the people we were trying to reach.

One of the first things I requested was the company's customer persona segmentation.

A QA lead and an engineering manager, for example, face quite different problems and have different decisions to make, even when they're working on the same release.

I mapped each speaker's expertise against our target personas, identifying both their primary audience and adjacent audiences they could reach. This gave us a practical framework for editorial selection.

I built these distinctions into our content strategy for repurposing expert recordings. We were looking for stories that would help us attract a relevant audience, and we didn't need to turn every expert opinion into a product pitch to do that.

## Building a content engine for the audience we actually want

I designed a repeatable workflow covering the entire journey from the original recording to an accepted video package.

| Stage | Key decisions |
| --- | --- |
| Source assessment | Audience, objectives, formats and recording limitations |
| Candidate discovery | Identify potentially valuable standalone stories |
| Editorial selection | Evaluate candidates and compare alternatives |
| Production briefing | Specify cuts, subtitle scripts, hooks, visuals and deliverables |
| Editing and self-QA | Produce videos and verify compliance with the brief |
| Final acceptance | Review and approve deliverables for distribution |

Not every good story makes a good talking-head video. We also experimented with animated explanations and editorial graphics for recordings that didn't lend themselves to conventional video editing.

## AI-assisted workflows. QA included. For the AI, too.

I started by looking at existing AI-assisted production tools. Some of their technical components worked well, so there was no reason to reinvent them.

The editorial part, however, needed considerably more work. I developed my own selection methodology, scoring criteria, editorial standards and QA rules, refining the workflow automation as we worked with real recordings.

Did the agent always make the right choice? No. Sometimes a candidate from the reserve list was clearly better than one of its finalists. But reviewing well-prepared alternatives was considerably easier than starting with an hour-long recording.

Quality assurance presented another challenge. I didn't want to spend my time proofreading subtitles that I had already written in the production brief.

I developed an AI-assisted QA workflow covering subtitle accuracy, compliance with the brief, audio loudness, framing and export specifications.

But how do you know that the agent has actually checked something?

Sometimes it reported that it had checked the subtitles when it had actually transcribed the audio. Sometimes it inspected only occasional frames. I had to develop verification rules and evidence requirements for the automation itself.

The video editor hadn't worked with AI agents before. I introduced him to agent-based QA and adapted the toolkit so he could run the checks himself. We worked together to make the reports useful rather than just adding more information.

The idea was simple: if the finished video didn't match the brief, the editor should know about it before I received the file.

### Editorial judgment: when a dialogue beats a monologue

While working on a subsequent podcast episode, I came across a discussion about whether we should delegate human judgment to LLMs.

We could have extracted a provocative statement from one speaker. But another speaker offered an interesting counterargument: a calibrated LLM judge can make human judgment available to more people.

After exploring several versions, I selected a 46-second exchange between the two experts, preserving both positions.

Making it even shorter would have meant losing part of the argument. So I didn't.

## Beyond production: distribution strategy and performance monitoring

Once we could produce a complete video package in a week, another problem became apparent.

We could produce content faster than we could publish it.

There's a practical limit to how often someone can promote their own podcast on LinkedIn. Meanwhile, every invited guest has an audience of their own.

So we started changing our content distribution strategy: fewer pieces for the main host, more opportunities for guests to share content with their professional networks. I also proposed making better use of the company's own channels and existing video library.

But once we'd sent a video to a guest, how did we know whether it had actually been published? And how did we find out how well it performed?

For content asset management, I created a centralized registry with three connected views:

| View | What it tells us |
| --- | --- |
| Packages | What have we produced from each original recording? |
| Assets | What individual content pieces and platform-specific versions do we have? |
| Distribution | Who publishes them, where, when and with what results? |

The registry connected production briefs, finished files, audiences, publication responsibilities and performance checkpoints. It also provided a foundation for automated monitoring of publications and content performance.

## 41 deliverables, three weeks. What else changed?

The Developer Advocate no longer needed to prepare production briefs himself. I owned editorial selection, briefing and acceptance, while the video editor remained responsible for editing and rendering.

The Developer Advocate, Head of Content and VP of Marketing responded positively to the pilot, and we began implementing the workflow in regular production.

Our conversations changed, too. Instead of discussing individual edits and corrections, we could spend more time discussing content strategy, formats and distribution.

The next logical step would be to move upstream: instead of only working with existing recordings, bring topic ideas and editorial angles to our experts based on gaps in our content and questions our audience was asking.

The pilot demonstrated production capacity and a workable division of responsibilities. Audience growth and commercial impact had yet to be established.
